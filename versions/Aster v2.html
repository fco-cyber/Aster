    About this guide

    There is no single right way to be trans

    Start with three things worth knowing, then the words this guide uses, how it keeps your reading private, and how to read its evidence labels.

    Three things to know first

    1. Transition is yours to shape

      Transition, also called affirmation, means the steps you take to live as your gender. They can be social (your name, pronouns, clothes), medical (hormones, surgery) or legal (your documents). Some people want all of these, some want a few, and some want none. "Full transition" is whatever is complete for you.

    2. Hormones work slowly, and differently for everyone

      Most changes from gender-affirming hormones begin within months and keep developing for two to five years. The usual timings are doctors' estimates from clinical observation, and real bodies vary widely.

    3. Care and connection both matter

      In a Melbourne , starting testosterone straight away eased gender dysphoria and depression within three months compared with waiting. Support from other people also matters: social support and being affirmed are linked with better mental health.

    Words we use

    A few words, explained

    People describe themselves in many ways, and the words keep changing. Use the ones that feel right to you. These are the ones this guide uses.

    Trans and gender diverse

    Trans (transgender) describes people whose gender identity, their inner sense of being a woman, a man, both, neither or something else, differs from the sex they were assigned (recorded) at birth. Gender diverse widens this to include everyone whose gender doesn't fit those two boxes.

    Non-binary

    A gender that isn't only woman or only man. Some non-binary people want hormones or surgery, some want a few changes, and many want none.

    Gender dysphoria and euphoria

    Dysphoria is the distress that can come from a mismatch between your gender and your body or how others see you. Euphoria is its joyful counterpart: the sense of rightness when something fits.

    Feminising and masculinising

    Feminising hormone therapy is based on estradiol, the main oestrogen (a female-typical sex hormone). Masculinising therapy is based on testosterone, the main androgen (a male-typical sex hormone). Together they're called gender-affirming hormone therapy.

      The seven stages

      Your journey, stage by stage

      Transition isn't a straight line or a race. These seven stages are a map, not a rulebook: you might linger in one, skip another, move back and forth, or stop where you feel at home. Each stage has its own milestones worth marking.

      1. Wondering and exploringAny age, any length of time

        Many people spend months or years wondering about their gender before they say anything. You might try a different name online, notice which clothes or pronouns feel right, or read about other people's stories. Questioning doesn't commit you to anything.

        You might feel
        Curious, relieved, frightened, confused, or all of these in one day. Moments of , a quiet sense of "yes, this fits", are useful signposts.
        What helps
        Talking with other trans people, by phone at QLife or in a peer group such as Transgender Victoria's; a counsellor who works with gender diversity and won't push you either way; noting when you feel most yourself.
        Milestones
        Saying it out loud, even only to yourself. Telling one trusted person. Trying a name or pronouns somewhere safe.
      2. Finding careWeeks to months, depending on waits

        For most adults in Australia the first step is a GP. Many GPs now prescribe hormones, and the Australian Professional Association for Trans Health (AusPATH) lists experienced clinicians. In Victoria there are also specialist services: Equinox, a GP clinic for trans and gender diverse people; the Monash Health Gender Clinic, where waits can be long; and Austin Health's specialist hormone clinic, which works alongside your own doctor.

        Bring
        What you hope for, your questions, your medicines and health history, and a support person if you'd like one.
        Ask
        "Do you prescribe gender-affirming hormones, or can you refer me to someone who does?" and "Do you bulk-bill, and what will it cost?"
        Milestones
        Booking the first appointment. Being called by your name at the clinic for the first time.
      3. Deciding togetherOne to a few appointments

        Adults in Australia usually start hormones through : your doctor makes sure you understand what hormones can and can't do, the risks, which changes are permanent, and how they may affect fertility, and then you decide. A mental health assessment isn't required, but a psychologist or counsellor is involved if you'd like one, or if other health concerns need care first. You'll have baseline blood tests.

        Testosterone subsidised on the needs a one-off second opinion from an endocrinologist, urologist or sexual health physician, which can be by telehealth.

        Think about
        Fertility. Hormones can reduce fertility, possibly for good, so storing sperm or eggs beforehand is worth discussing even if you're unsure you'll ever want children. More on fertility.
        Milestones
        Signing your consent. Your first blood test. Making your fertility decision, whatever it is.
      4. Starting hormonesThe first 3 months

        The first changes are often felt before they're seen. On estradiol, sexual desire and spontaneous erections usually lessen within one to three months, and some people notice tender nipples or breasts. On testosterone, skin often becomes oilier, sex drive often rises, and periods may begin to change. Your first review and blood tests usually come at around three months.

        You might feel
        Excited, impatient, or worried that nothing is happening. Early weeks are normal and slow; the biggest changes come later.
        Milestones
        Your first dose. Your first three-month check.
      5. Visible changeAbout 3 to 12 months

        This is when many people start to see themselves in the mirror. On estradiol, breasts grow (most of the first year's growth happens in the first six months), skin softens and fat begins to move toward the hips. On testosterone, the voice deepens, facial and body hair start to grow, muscle builds, and for most people periods have stopped by 12 months. Reviews continue every three to four months.

        Alongside
        Many people change their name and pronouns at work or study, start voice training, or update documents in this period.
        Milestones
        A voice that feels like yours. Periods stopping. A new name on your Medicare card. A day of not thinking about your gender at all.
      6. Growing into yourselfAbout 1 to 3 years

        Changes continue, more gently. Breast volume keeps increasing slowly for at least three years, and facial hair and body shape keep developing for two to five years. Once things are stable, check-ups usually drop to once a year. If you're considering surgery, this is when many people meet surgeons, and some operations ask for about six months of stable hormones first.

        You might feel
        Calmer, and also more aware of what hormones can't change, such as height or bone structure. Both are normal.
        Milestones
        One year on hormones. A new birth certificate. A first surgical consultation, if that's your path.
      7. Settled, and ageing wellThree years and beyond

        For many people, transition becomes less of a project and more of a life. Care continues: most people keep taking hormones long term, with yearly blood tests and check-ups, attention to bone and heart health, and cancer screening for the body you have now.

        Keep in mind
        If your testes or ovaries are removed, you'll need to keep taking hormones to protect your bones. Health checks and screening.
        Milestones
        Five years. Screening up to date. Helping someone else find their way.

      Hormones

      How hormones work

      Gender-affirming hormone therapy uses the body's own messengers to reshape it, slowly, toward your gender. Knowing how the system works makes it easier to understand the medicines, the blood tests and the timeline.

      The control loop in your body

      Sex hormones are controlled by a loop between your brain and your gonads (the testes or ovaries). The hypothalamus, deep in the brain, sends a signal to the . The pituitary then sends two hormones, LH and FSH, through the blood to the gonads, which make testosterone or estradiol. When those levels rise, the brain eases off its signal, a thermostat-like brake called . Hormone therapy works by adding the hormone you want and, where needed, lowering or blocking the one you don't.

      The medicines

      Feminising

      Estradiol, usually with an anti-androgen

      Estradiol (the main oestrogen) can be a skin patch, a gel rubbed in daily, or a daily tablet; some people use an implant under the skin, which in Australia is a specially made (compounded) product rather than a TGA-approved one. Patches and gel may be preferred if you have a higher risk of blood clots, for example if you smoke, are over 45, live with obesity or have a clotting condition, because unlike tablets they don't appear to raise clot risk.

      An is usually added to bring testosterone down: in Australia, at a low dose, or . Low-dose cyproterone (10 mg a day) lowered testosterone as well as higher doses, with fewer side effects. If the testes are removed, anti-androgens are no longer needed.

      Some people add progesterone. The evidence for benefit is limited, so it's reviewed after 3 to 6 months.

      Masculinising

      Testosterone

      Testosterone can be an injection into a muscle, either every 2 to 4 weeks or, in the long-acting form, usually every 10 to 14 weeks, or a gel or cream applied to the skin each day.

      In the skin and hair roots, some testosterone is turned into a stronger form, , which drives facial hair and acne. A small amount is turned into estradiol by , which helps keep bones strong. The ovaries go quiet and periods usually stop, but testosterone is not contraception.

      Testosterone subsidised on the PBS needs a one-off second opinion from an endocrinologist, urologist or sexual health physician.

      Non-binary and lower-dose paths. Some people want only some changes, or want them more slowly. Doses and timing can be adjusted, but changes can't be fully picked one by one, because several come together. Research on non-binary people's hormone care is still limited, so it's worked out individually. Whatever the dose, levels need to stay high enough to protect your bones.

      Fertility and contraception

      Fertility can fall, possibly for good

      Hormone therapy may reduce fertility and could make it permanent, though the evidence on how often is limited. Stopping hormones may restore it, but that isn't guaranteed. You'll be offered fertility counselling before you start; it's your choice whether to store sperm or eggs.

      If you tuck, pausing for three to four months before giving sperm samples may help: tucking has been linked with lower sperm quality.

      Testosterone is not contraception

      Pregnancy can happen on testosterone, and testosterone can harm a developing baby (it's a ). If you have a uterus and ovaries and have sex that could lead to pregnancy, use contraception before, during and for a while after testosterone.

      Estradiol isn't contraception either: sperm production falls slowly and unpredictably, so pregnancy with a partner is still possible.

      Starting in Australia: informed consent

      For adults, Australian standards treat starting hormones as a decision you make with your doctor once you understand it, not something you have to earn. It is still a careful process.

      1. Talk about your goals. What you hope will change, what worries you, and what you'd like to keep.
      2. Go through benefits, risks and limits. Including the permanent changes above, fertility, and your own health history, such as smoking or clotting problems.
      3. Baseline tests. Blood tests for blood count, kidneys, liver and hormone levels, and a blood pressure check.
      4. Sign a consent form, often AusPATH's, and start at a dose that is adjusted gradually.
      5. Review every 3 to 4 months in the first year, then once a year when things are stable.

      You can pause, change or stop at any time. Stopping isn't failure, and isn't the same as regret.

      Bodies differ

      Change varies widely

      How much breast growth, facial hair or voice change you get varies widely between people, much as it did in puberty. In one study, blood estradiol levels didn't predict how much breast growth happened in the first year.

      Patience

      Slow is normal

      Breast growth is mostly in the first six months, then volume keeps creeping up for at least three years. Facial hair keeps filling in for four to five years. In studies, blood hormone levels didn't predict how much breast growth or voice change people had, and higher doses of cyproterone brought more side effects without lowering testosterone further.

      Comparison

      Online timelines aren't yours

      Photo timelines online are usually the most dramatic cases. Comparing with your own photos from a few months ago is kinder and more accurate.

      What stays

      Bones don't change shape

      Once growth plates have closed in adulthood, hormones don't change height, the size of hands and feet, the width of the shoulders and hips, or the shape of the jaw. Surgery, voice training, hair removal and clothing can do things hormones can't.

      Hormones

      Staying safe on hormones

      With regular checks, gender-affirming hormones are effective and, in the short term, relatively safe. Long-term research is still growing, so the checks matter. Here is what's monitored, the risks worth knowing, and the signs that need help quickly.

      Signs that need help

      Call 000 now

      • Chest pain or pressure, or sudden breathlessness
      • Coughing up blood
      • A face that droops, an arm that's weak, or speech that's slurred (signs of stroke)
      • A sudden, severe headache, especially with changes in vision or weakness
      • Sudden or severe pain in the belly, pelvis, groin or a testis, especially if you could be pregnant
      • Very heavy bleeding with dizziness or fainting

      These can be signs of a blood clot in the lung, a stroke, or another emergency such as a pregnancy outside the womb or a twisted testis.

      See a doctor straight away

      • Pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one calf or leg, which can be a clot in a deep vein
      • New pain or cramping low in the pelvis, especially if you could be pregnant
      • A headache that won't go away, with blurred vision
      • Very heavy bleeding

      Your GP or an after-hours clinic, the same day. If it's severe or getting worse, call 000.

      Risks worth knowing

      Blood clots

      The main risk to know

      In a large US health-plan study, transfeminine people had about 4 extra clots per 1,000 people over 2 years, and about 17 extra per 1,000 over 8 years, compared with cisgender men; among those who started hormones during the study, the gap was larger and strokes were also more common. Across 18 studies about 2% had a clot, more often with older age and longer use. Smoking, being over 45, obesity and clotting conditions raise the risk, which is why patches or gel may be suggested.

      Cyproterone

      Keep the dose low

      were more common in trans women than in the general population; most had used cyproterone for years, often at higher doses. Whether low doses avoid this isn't known yet. Low doses lower testosterone just as well, with fewer side effects such as raised . Tell your doctor about new headaches or changes in vision.

      Breast and prostate

      Screening for the body you have

      Breast cancer was more common in trans women than in cisgender men, but less common than in cisgender women. Prostate cancer was about one-fifth as common as in cisgender men, and the prostate stays after vaginoplasty.

      Day to day

      Common side effects

      Breast tenderness, tiredness, mood changes, and sometimes painful erections. Spironolactone can make you pass more urine and raise potassium.

      Thicker blood

      Red cells rise

      Red cells rise most in the first year, but high levels can first appear years later. In a study that followed 1,073 trans men for up to 20 years, the chance of developing a haematocrit above 0.50 () was about 1 in 10 after one year and nearly 4 in 10 after ten. Smoking roughly doubled the risk; long-acting injections, higher body weight, older age at starting and some lung conditions also raised it. Keep up blood tests for as long as you take testosterone; stopping smoking or switching to gel can help.

      Skin and hair

      Acne, then hair

      Acne usually peaks around 6 months and settles for most people. After about 10 years, about a third had mild and a third moderate to severe male-pattern hair loss.

      Heart and sleep

      Blood pressure, cholesterol, snoring

      Testosterone can raise blood pressure and cholesterol, and can bring on or worsen sleep apnoea (breathing pauses during sleep). Research on heart attack and stroke with testosterone is still mixed.

      Pelvis

      Dryness and pain

      Vaginal dryness is common, and so is new pelvic pain: more than 70% report it in surveys. It's often cramping and low in the pelvis, starting around a year in, and it deserves a check rather than putting up with it. Testosterone also harms a developing baby, so contraception matters.

      Three things that lower risk

      Stopping smoking

      Smoking raises the risk of clots on estradiol and roughly doubles the risk of thick blood on testosterone. Quitline (13 7848) is free, and LGBTIQA+ peers work in the service.

      Keeping your appointments

      Most problems, such as a rising red-cell count or potassium, show up on blood tests before you feel them.

      Hormones with a prescription

      Hormones bought online without a prescription may not contain what the label says, and without blood tests problems go unnoticed. If cost or waiting is the barrier, an informed-consent GP may be quicker than you think. If you already use them, tell your doctor: you deserve safe care, not judgement.

      Body

      Body and movement

      Hormones change muscle, fat and bone, and so they change how your body moves, feels and recovers. Knowing what's happening under the skin helps you train well, stay comfortable and avoid injury.

      Muscle and strength

      On testosterone

      Strength comes quickly

      In the first year, thigh muscle volume rose by about 15% in a small Swedish study, and grip strength rose by about 6 kg on average in a larger European one. Men's-standard fitness results were usually matched within a year.

      For training: muscles get stronger faster than adapt, especially while hormones are rising. Building load gradually over months, particularly for running, jumping and heavy lifting, gives tendons time to catch up. This comes from studies of teenagers going through puberty, so it's an educated extrapolation.

      On estradiol

      Muscle eases, slowly

      Thigh muscle volume fell by about 5% in a year while strength was largely kept, and grip strength fell by about 2 kg. Whether strength keeps falling after the first year isn't clear, but on average it stays above cisgender women's after three years.

      For training: haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, falls to the usual female range within about four months, so hard efforts may feel tougher at first. Keep strength training: it protects muscle and bone. If hard sessions feel tougher, it's fine to lower the load for a while.

      Fat and body shape

      Hormones move fat rather than simply adding or removing it. On estradiol, fat builds mostly on the hips and thighs, and the hip measurement grew by about 3 cm in a year. On testosterone, fat leaves the hips and thighs while the waist stays about the same, so the shape becomes straighter. These changes keep developing for two to five years.

      Changes in body shape can bring up strong feelings about food and weight. If eating is becoming a way to control your shape, please talk to your GP; body dissatisfaction is a known risk for disordered eating in trans people, and support helps.

      Bones

      Hormones protect bone

      In the first year of hormones, rose: in trans women at the spine (about 4%) and hip, and in trans men at the hip. Sex hormones are what keep bones strong, so the risk comes from long stretches without them.

      A gap before starting

      Before hormones, trans women in one small study had lower bone density, less muscle and lower vitamin D than other men, which the authors linked to being less active. Being active supports bone and muscle whenever you start.

      Later in life

      Trans women over 50 had more fractures than men their age, about the same as women their age. Trans men had no higher risk. If your testes or ovaries are removed, keep taking hormones: bone loss is likely when levels stay low.

      What helps your bones: strength training at least twice a week and weight-bearing activity such as walking, food rich in calcium, some safe sun for vitamin D, not smoking, and taking your hormones consistently.

      Joints, flexibility and hypermobility

      In a large set of US health records, conditions were diagnosed far more often in trans and gender diverse people than in cisgender people, whether or not they used hormones. The reason isn't known, and a records study can't show how common it really is, but it's worth knowing.

      • If your joints bend further than most people's and you get frequent sprains, aches or clicking, mention it to your GP, physiotherapist or osteopath.
      • Strength and control exercises usually help hypermobile joints more than extra stretching does.
      • On testosterone, rapid strength gains can tempt you to lift more than your joints and tendons are ready for: progress steadily.

      Posture, the chest and the shoulders

      Some people who are uncomfortable with their chest round their shoulders and drop their head to hide it, sometimes for years, which can stiffen the upper back and the front of the chest. Binding adds pressure on the ribs and breathing muscles (see Binding and tucking).

      After top surgery, muscle and joint problems are common and often missed. In a Canadian survey, most people had altered chest sensation (82%) and chest tightness (71%), and over half had stiff shoulders (56%). Most were mild, apart from changed sensation, but only 29% said they had fully settled, and only 28% had sought treatment such as physiotherapy.

      Gentle upper-back mobility, slow deep breathing into the lower ribs, and exercises that strengthen the muscles between the shoulder blades can all help, once any surgical restrictions have ended. A physiotherapist or osteopath can tailor this to you.

      Your voice

      On testosterone

      A deeper voice, for good

      Average speaking dropped clearly within three months and kept falling to a year, reaching the typical male range for most. About a quarter had an unstable or tired voice and had voice therapy. The change is permanent.

      On estradiol

      Training, not hormones

      Estradiol doesn't change the voice. Voice training with a speech pathologist raised average pitch by about 31 Hz, with roughly 80 to 85% of people satisfied, and changes in pitch and were mostly kept a year later. Surgery can raise pitch further but can make the voice quieter.

      Protect your voice as you train: practise little and often, keep your throat relaxed, and stop if it hurts or goes hoarse. In Victoria, the La Trobe University voice clinic can be reached through the Monash Health Gender Clinic, though waits are long; private speech pathologists also offer gender-affirming voice training.

      Moving more, comfortably

      In a UK study, trans people were, on average, less active than cisgender people, and those on hormones were more active than those not yet on them. Across studies, the biggest barrier to sport and exercise was not having comfortable, inclusive spaces, such as changing rooms.

      The guideline

      Australian adults are advised to be active on most days, with muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week and balance and mobility work on 3. Any activity is better than none.

      Ways around the barriers

      Home or park workouts, walking or running groups, gyms with private changing areas, LGBTIQA+ sports clubs, swimming in a rash vest and board shorts, or quieter session times.

      Competitive sport

      Rules differ by sport and level, and are changing. Ask your club or the sport's governing body. In Victoria, the Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission has a guideline on including trans and gender diverse people in sport.

      The pelvis and pelvic floor

      The is the sling of muscle at the base of the pelvis. On testosterone, new pelvic pain is very common, reported by more than 70% in surveys; it's often cramping and low in the pelvis, starting around a year in. It should be checked by a GP or gynaecologist, and a pelvic health physiotherapist may help. Before vaginoplasty, pelvic floor problems are common: in one US program, 42% had them, and people who saw a pelvic floor physiotherapist both before and after surgery had far fewer problems afterwards.

      Body

      Binding, tucking and packing

      For many people these everyday practices make it possible to leave the house feeling like themselves. They bring real relief and some real wear on the body. Here is how they affect you, and how to make them gentler.

      Chest binding

      Binding means flattening the chest with a tight garment, usually a purpose-made . In a US survey of 13- to 24-year-olds, people who bound were misgendered less often, and across studies people who bind describe less chest dysphoria and better mood.

      97%

      of 1,800 adults who bound reported at least one unwanted effect, such as pain, overheating, skin problems or breathlessness.

      Days

      per week was the factor most consistently linked with problems: binding on more days meant more symptoms.

      15%

      of people who bound had sought care related to binding, though 89% had symptoms. Feeling safe to raise it with a clinician went with about twice the odds of seeking care.

      What binding does to breathing

      When you breathe in, the diaphragm, the dome of muscle under the lungs, flattens downward. At the same time the lower ribs swing out to the sides like the handle of a bucket, and the upper ribs and breastbone lift forward like an old water-pump handle. A tight binder holds the ribs in, so each breath is shallower and the muscles of the neck and shoulders work harder to help. That extra work is one reason binding can bring neck, shoulder and upper-back pain.

      Gentler binding

      There are no trials of safer binding, so this advice comes from the one large survey and from clinicians' and community experience.

      1. Bind fewer days when you can, and take the binder off for breaks during the day.
      2. Choose your true size, never smaller. If you can't take a full, comfortable breath, or it hurts, it's too tight.
      3. Don't sleep in it. Your ribs, skin and lungs need the night off.
      4. Swap it for exercise. A sports compression top or similar is gentler when you're breathing hard.
      5. Skip bandages, tape and plastic wrap. They aren't made for binding, can tighten as you move and can damage skin.
      6. Look after your skin. Keep it dry, wash the binder often, and give rashes or broken skin time to heal.
      7. Get help early for pain that lasts, rib or chest pain, numbness, breathlessness, or skin that breaks down. You won't be told off.
      A five-minute binder breakGentle movements for when the binder comes off

      General movements, not a treatment. Stop anything that hurts, and ask a physiotherapist or osteopath to tailor them for you.

      1. Side-rib breathing. Rest your hands on your lower ribs. Breathe in slowly through your nose and feel the ribs widen into your hands; breathe out slowly. Five breaths.
      2. Doorway chest opener. Forearms on a door frame at shoulder height, step gently forward until you feel a stretch across the front of the chest. Hold 20 to 30 seconds.
      3. Shoulder-blade squeeze. Draw your shoulder blades back and down, hold for 5 seconds, relax. Ten times.
      4. Upper-back extension. Sitting tall, clasp your hands behind your head and gently lift your breastbone toward the ceiling. Five slow repeats.
      5. Neck release. Slowly tip one ear toward the shoulder, breathe out, and come back. Three each side.

      Tucking

      positions the penis and testes back between the legs for a flatter look, often with a gaff (firm, close-fitting underwear) or tape. Reported problems include itching, skin irritation and infections, and, rarely, testicular torsion: a testis twisting on its cord, which is an emergency. Extensive tucking has also been linked with lower sperm quality.

      • A gaff or tucking underwear is gentler on skin than tape. If you use tape, use skin-safe medical tape, never duct tape, and remove it slowly.
      • Take breaks, keep the area clean and dry, and don't hold on when you need to pass urine.
      • If you might want to store sperm, pausing tucking for three to four months beforehand may help.

      Sudden, severe pain or swelling in a testis needs emergency care now. Untuck, and go to an emergency department or call 000. Testicular torsion needs treatment within hours.

      Packing and padding

      (wearing a soft prosthetic in your underwear), breast forms and padding are low risk. Keep skin clean and dry, choose breathable materials, and give the skin a break if it gets sore or rubbed.

      Mind

      Mind and emotions

      Transition can bring relief and joy, and hard days too. Hormones change how feelings feel for some people, and how others treat you shapes your mental health as much as anything happening in your body. Here is what to expect, and what helps.

      How hormones can feel

      On estradiol

      Softer edges

      Many people describe feeling calmer, with emotions closer to the surface, so tears may come more easily. Mood on estradiol is little studied, so this comes mostly from clinical experience.

      Sex drive and spontaneous erections usually lessen in the first three months. By three years, desire for sex with a partner was, on average, higher than before starting.

      On testosterone

      A stronger drive, not more anger

      Sex drive often rises in the first months, which can feel intense. By three years, desire for sex with a partner was, on average, back near where it started, while desire for solo sex stayed higher.

      In a UK study that followed people for a year, testosterone did not increase self-reported aggression. Aggression went with anxiety, not with hormone levels.

      Either way

      Waiting is hard too

      Waiting for appointments, waiting for changes and seeing them begin can all stir up strong feelings. Low mood that lasts more than two weeks, or any thoughts of harming yourself, are worth raising with your GP straight away.

      What studies show about mental health

      3 mo

      In a Melbourne trial with 64 adults, starting testosterone straight away eased gender dysphoria, depression and suicidal thoughts within three months, compared with waiting.

      18 mo

      At a UK gender clinic, depression fell clearly after 18 months on hormones, while anxiety did not change significantly. People with more social support improved more.

      20

      studies in one review linked hormones with better quality of life and less depression and anxiety, though the authors rated their certainty as limited because of how the studies were designed.

      Hormones ease gender distress for most people, but they aren't a cure for everything. Many people still need support for anxiety, past trauma or ordinary life stress. Needing that support doesn't mean transition isn't working.

      Dysphoria and euphoria

      When dysphoria is loud

      can come in waves: around mirrors, photos, certain clothes, being misgendered, or for no clear reason. Some things people find help on hard days:

      • Clothes, binding or padding that feel right, worn safely
      • Less time with mirrors or photos for a while
      • A message to someone who sees you as you are
      • Something absorbing: a walk, music, a task for your hands
      • Reminding yourself the feeling will pass, even if the reason doesn't

      Noticing euphoria

      is the joyful sense of rightness when something fits. People describe it coming from within, from their body or clothes, or from being seen correctly by others.

      Euphoria is worth noticing, not just dysphoria: it shows you what to move toward. You can record those moments in My milestones.

      The stress of prejudice

      Being trans is not a mental illness. Trans people's higher rates of anxiety and depression are strongly linked to how they're treated. This is called : ongoing, extra stress from prejudice, piled on top of everyday life. It comes from outside, such as rejection, discrimination or violence, and then works its way inside, as expecting rejection, hiding, or turning others' prejudice on yourself (). Surveys show the link but can't show how much of the difference it explains. Support, community and pride are linked with softer effects.

      What protects your mental health

      People who back you

      Support from family, friends and partners was linked with a bigger fall in depression on hormones and, in a Canadian survey, with a much lower risk of suicidal thoughts.

      Your name, used

      In a US study of young people, each extra place they could use their chosen name (home, school, work, friends) was linked with less depression and fewer suicidal thoughts. It's likely to matter for adults too, though adults weren't studied.

      Documents that match

      People whose identity documents all showed their name and gender had about a third less serious psychological distress than those with none that matched. Changing your documents.

      Other trans people

      In a US survey, support from other trans people softened the link between stigma and distress. Peer groups, online communities and trans-led services can all offer this.

      Care when you need it

      Getting wanted medical care, when people needed it, was linked with much lower risk of suicidal thoughts in a Canadian survey, and starting sooner helped in the Melbourne trial.

      Pride

      Connection to community and pride in who you are are named as sources of resilience in the gender minority stress model.

      The harder numbersAbout depression, abuse and discrimination among trans Australians. Opens only if you choose; it's fine to skip.

      These figures describe what prejudice does to people, not who trans people are. They come from a national survey of 928 trans adults in 2017 and 2018.

      73%

      had been diagnosed with depression at some point, and 67% with anxiety.

      63%

      had experienced verbal abuse, and 22% physical assault.

      26%

      had faced discrimination in health care. Better training for doctors was their top priority.

      In a Canadian survey, social support, less transphobia, documents that matched and access to wanted medical care were each linked with much lower risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts. In other words, much of this may be changeable.

      If reading this has stirred things up, you can talk to someone now. Lifeline is on 13 11 14 at any hour, and QLife on 1800 184 527 (3 pm to 9 pm). In danger, call 000. More support.

      Autism, ADHD and neurodiversity

      Trans and gender diverse people are more likely than others to be autistic or to have ADHD or another difference. In the Australian survey, 15% reported autism and 11% ADHD. Being neurodivergent doesn't make your gender less real. It can help to tell your clinicians what makes appointments easier for you, such as written information, extra time or a quiet waiting space.

      Body image and eating

      Feeling unhappy with your body is at the heart of the distress many trans people feel, and it may put some at risk of disordered eating. Gender-affirming care tended to improve how people felt about their bodies.

      If food, weight or exercise is starting to feel like a way to control your shape, please talk to your GP, or call the Butterfly National Helpline on 1800 33 4673 (8 am to midnight, every day). Support works, and you don't need to be very unwell to ask.

      Finding the right counsellor

      Not a gatekeeper

      Adults in Australia don't need a mental health assessment to start hormones. A counsellor or psychologist is there because you want support, or if other concerns need care first.

      Look for experience

      Ask whether they've worked with trans and gender diverse people, and how. A good counsellor follows your lead rather than steering you toward or away from transition. The AusPATH directory lists affirming clinicians; your GP can refer you for Medicare-subsidised psychology sessions.

      Conversion practices are banned

      In Victoria it's against the law to try to change or suppress someone's gender identity. If it has happened to you, you can report it to the Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission.

      Doubts, pausing and changing course

      Doubt is human. Doubting a step, such as a dose or an operation, isn't the same as doubting who you are, and both are fine to talk through with your clinicians.

      Most people continue

      In a US study of children and spouses of military members (average age 19), 70% were still filling hormone prescriptions four years after starting (64% of those who started as adults). Some may have continued outside that health system. Why others stopped wasn't recorded, and stopping isn't the same as regret.

      Many reasons for stopping

      In a large US survey, 13% had ever stopped or gone back, even briefly, and most of them named outside pressures such as family or stigma. In a smaller survey of 100 people who detransitioned, reasons included becoming more comfortable with their birth sex, health concerns, and coming to see their distress as caused by something else.

      Care either way

      If you're thinking about stopping or changing course, tell your doctor: some changes ease and some don't, and if your testes or ovaries have been removed you'll still need hormones to protect your bones. You deserve the same respect whichever way you go.

      Life and people

      Daily life and habits

      The everyday side of care: taking your hormones well, sex and sexual health, skin and hair, sleep and drinking, and travelling with your medicines.

      Taking your hormones well

      Patches

      A new spot each time

      Follow the leaflet for where and how often to change them. Moving each new patch to a different spot is gentler on your skin. If one falls off, ask your pharmacist what to do.

      Gels

      Keep it to yourself

      Testosterone gel can pass to other people through skin contact. Wash your hands after applying, let it dry and cover the area with clothing. Before close skin contact, shower or wash the area with soap and water, and leave at least 6 hours between applying it and intimate contact. Keep children, anyone pregnant or breastfeeding, and pets away from the treated skin; if someone touches it, wash their skin with soap and water. For estradiol gel, ask your pharmacist about the same care.

      Injections

      Safe and steady

      Use a new needle every time, change the spot you inject, and put used needles in a sharps container; your pharmacy or council can tell you where to return it. A practice nurse can teach you, or give the injection for you.

      Tablets

      Same time, every day

      A weekly pill box or a phone reminder helps. If you miss a dose of any hormone, check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist; don't double up unless you've been told to.

      Reorder before you run low, because your pharmacy may need to order your medicine in. Keep every blood-test appointment: they're how problems are caught early. Your regular checks.

      Sex, intimacy and sexual health

      Your sex life may change

      On estradiol, desire and spontaneous erections usually lessen within months; on testosterone, desire often rises early. Both tend to settle over the following years. Talking with partners about what feels good now, which may be different, helps.

      Dryness on testosterone

      Dryness, irritation or soreness of the vagina and vulva on testosterone is very common, estimated at 64 to 78% within five years. Lubricant and moisturisers help, and a doctor can check for infection and discuss a low-dose vaginal estrogen that acts locally. Evidence for treatment is still limited.

      Protecting yourself

      If you have new partners, regular sexual health checks and condoms protect you. PrEP, a medicine that prevents HIV, is recommended as a daily tablet for trans women and for trans men who have vaginal sex; the on-demand way of taking it hasn't been shown to work for them.

      Tell a sexual health clinic about your body, including any surgery, so they offer the right tests. Neither testosterone nor estradiol is contraception: fertility and contraception.

      Skin and hair

      Testosterone

      Acne, then calmer skin

      Oilier skin and acne usually peak around six months and settle for most people. A gentle cleanser and non-greasy moisturiser help; if acne is painful, scarring or getting you down, your GP has effective treatments.

      Testosterone

      Scalp hair

      After about 10 years on testosterone, a third had mild and a third moderate to severe male-pattern hair loss. If it bothers you, talk to your GP about the options and how they might affect other changes.

      Estradiol

      Facial and body hair

      Estradiol slows facial and body hair growth but rarely stops it. Laser heats the dark pigment in hair, so it works best on dark hair and usually reduces rather than removes it; electrolysis destroys hair roots one at a time and works on any colour. Both take many sessions.

      Before genital surgery

      Hair removal first

      For vaginoplasty, surgeons usually ask for hair to be cleared from the skin that will line the vagina, often with electrolysis over several months, so start early. Ask your surgeon whether your operation needs this too.

      Sleep, food and drink

      Sleep

      Adults need 7 to 9 hours of good-quality sleep. On testosterone, new or louder snoring, or feeling sleepy in the day, can be a sign of sleep apnoea and is worth mentioning.

      Food

      Appetite and weight can shift as hormones move fat and muscle around, so the scales don't tell the whole story. Regular, varied meals with enough protein and calcium support your muscles and bones.

      Alcohol, smoking and vaping

      Australian guidelines advise no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any day. Smoking raises the risk of clots on estradiol and of thick blood on testosterone; Quitline (13 7848) has LGBTIQA+ peers working in the service.

      Travelling with your medicines

      1. Get a letter from your doctor naming each medicine and dose and saying it's for your personal use, and keep your prescriptions with you.
      2. Keep medicines in their original packaging with the pharmacy label, and pack them, with any needles, in your hand luggage. Take enough for the whole trip.
      3. Check your destination. Some medicines are restricted overseas, and some countries criminalise LGBTQIA+ people, including laws aimed at people who dress as another gender. Read Smartraveller's advice for each country before you book.
      4. Think about your passport. Australian passports can show M, F or X, but some countries don't recognise X.

      Family, friends and the world

      Living as yourself happens among other people. Here is help with telling people, relationships, work and study, your rights in Australia, and changing your name and documents.

      Telling people

      Coming out is yours to pace. There's no deadline and no one you have to tell first. A few things many people find useful:

      1. Start where it feels safest. Often that's one person you expect to be supportive, who can then back you up with others.
      2. Think about safety first if you depend on someone for housing, money or care, or if there's been violence at home. Rainbow Door (1800 729 367) can help you plan, and Safe Steps (1800 015 188) answers day and night.
      3. Choose the moment and the way. Face to face, by phone or in a letter or message are all fine. Writing it down first can help you say what matters most.
      4. Have support lined up afterwards, whatever happens: a friend, a peer group, or QLife on 1800 184 527.
      5. Give people time. First reactions aren't always final ones. You don't owe anyone a debate about who you are.
      Ways to start the conversationWords some people have used; make them your own
      • "There's something important about me I'd like you to know, and I'm telling you because you matter to me."
      • "I've known for a while that I'm trans. I'm going to start living as myself, and I'd love you to be part of that."
      • "My name is now Sam, and I use they/them. It might take practice, and that's OK. What matters is that you try."
      • "You don't have to understand everything today. I'm happy to answer questions, and there's a guide for families I can share."

      Partners and relationships

      Transition changes things for partners too. Some relationships grow closer, some change shape, and some end, and each of those can be right. Honest conversation about what's changing in your body, your sex life and how you're seen as a couple helps. Couples counselling with someone experienced in gender diversity can help both of you, and partners can call QLife as well.

      Work and study

      The law is on your side

      Federal law has protected people from discrimination because of gender identity since 2013, including at work and in education, and Victoria's Equal Opportunity Act protects gender identity too.

      A transition plan

      Many workplaces and universities will agree a plan with you: who is told and how, when your name changes on email and records, and how questions are handled. You decide how much to share.

      If things go wrong

      Keep a dated note of what happened and who was there. Talk to HR, your union or student support, and if that doesn't resolve it, you can complain to the Victorian or Australian human rights commission.

      When people get it wrong

      Being misgendered or called by your old name (deadnamed) hurts, and it can happen even with people who love you. It can help to decide ahead of time how you'll respond in different places: a quick correction ("It's Sam, actually"), letting it pass when you're not safe or not up to it, or a private word later. You get to choose, every time.

      Your name and documents

      Documents that match who you are make everyday life easier, and they're linked with less distress. These are the usual steps for adults in Victoria. Tick them off as you go; the list stays on this device.

      Your rights, in short

      • Discrimination because of gender identity is against federal law, and against Victorian law, in work, education, housing, goods and services.

      Surgery

      Surgery: your options

      Surgery is one option among many, not a final step everyone takes. Some people want several operations, some one, and many none. Here are the main options, what results are really like, and how to prepare.

      You don't need surgery to be who you are. In Victoria you can change the sex on your birth certificate without any surgery or medical evidence, and "full transition" is whatever is complete for you.

      What surgeons usually look for

      International standards suggest these for adults having gender-affirming surgery. Your surgeon will tell you what assessments or letters they need.

      • Your gender incongruence is marked and has lasted over time.
      • Other things that could explain the distress have been considered.
      • You can give informed consent, and you understand how the operation affects fertility.
      • Other physical or mental health conditions that could affect surgery or recovery have been assessed and are being cared for.
      • For some operations, about six months of stable hormone treatment first, unless hormones aren't wanted or aren't medically suitable.

      Numbers come from reviews that pooled many surgical studies. Results vary between techniques and surgeons, so ask your surgeon for their own. Recovery week by week is in Surgery: recovery.

      Chest reconstruction (top surgery)Removes breast tissue and shapes a flatter chest

      removes most breast tissue and reshapes the chest. For larger chests the usual method is a double incision, with the nipples resized and placed back as skin grafts; smaller chests can sometimes be done through a small cut around the nipple.

      Results
      Across 8 studies, 6% of operated sides needed an urgent return to theatre for bleeding under the skin (), and about a quarter of sides (26.5%) needed a further operation later. Double incision with nipple grafts had fewer urgent returns than two other methods.

      There's no set screening after top surgery. Be chest aware: get to know how your chest looks and feels, see your GP about any changes, and ask your surgeon whether any breast tissue was left.

      Implant type and size, where the cut will be, scar care, and how often implants need checking or replacing over the years. If you go for breast screening, tell the staff you have implants.

      ~1%

      Preparing well

      1. Stop smoking and vaping, ideally months ahead. Nicotine narrows blood vessels and slows healing, and many surgeons won't operate on people who smoke. Quitline: 13 7848.

      4. Ask about your hormones around surgery. Some surgeons pause estradiol to lower clot risk; in one US centre, continuing it through vaginoplasty wasn't linked with more clots. Follow your own team's plan.
      5. Decide about fertility before any operation that removes the testes, ovaries or uterus.
      6. Line up help at home for the first weeks: lifting, shopping, driving, and company. Plan time off work, using the recovery times in the next section.
      7. Plan the costs and follow-up. Ask about the full cost, including hospital and anaesthetist fees, what Medicare and private health insurance cover, and any public options in your state. If you go interstate or overseas, plan who will look after you when you're home.

      • Which technique do you recommend for me, and why?
      • What do I need to do beforehand: hormones, hair removal, weight, smoking?
      • How long will I be in hospital, off work, and unable to drive, lift, exercise or have sex?
      • Who do I call after hours if something seems wrong?
      • What does follow-up look like, and for how long?
      • What is the total cost, and what if I need a revision?

        • Not being able to pass urine
        • A flap, nipple graft or new penis that changes colour (pale, blue or dark) or feels cold
        • Pain, swelling or warmth in one calf
        • A temperature of 38 °C or more, or shivers
        • Redness spreading from the wound, or pus or a bad smell

        If you can't reach them, or it's after hours, go to an emergency department and take your surgery details.

        Usual, and worth mentioning

        • Bruising and swelling that slowly ease
        • Numbness or tingling near the scars
        • Tiredness for several weeks
        • Feeling flat or tearful

        Raise these at your follow-up visit.

        Recovering well

        Walk early, little and often

        Short, gentle walks from the first days, as your team allows, help prevent blood clots and stiffness. Slow, deep breaths a few times an hour help your lungs.

        Stay ahead of pain

        Take pain relief as prescribed rather than waiting for pain to build. Strong pain medicines often cause constipation, so drink water and ask about a gentle laxative.

        Scars mature slowly

        Scars are usually red and firm at first and fade over 12 to 18 months. Once the wound has fully healed, your team may suggest massage, silicone gel or sheets, and protection from the sun.

        Feelings after surgery

        Many people feel flat, tearful or unsettled in the weeks after a big operation, even one they wanted very much: tiredness, pain and being stuck at home all play a part. It usually passes. If it doesn't, or if you feel unsafe, tell your GP or team.

        Moving again after top surgery

        • Until about 4 weeks: gentle everyday arm movement, keeping your elbows below shoulder height.
        • Until 4 to 6 weeks: nothing heavier than about 2 kg, and no strenuous exercise.
        • From about 4 weeks: slow, comfortable reaching and gentle shoulder and upper-back mobility, as your surgeon allows. Avoid anything that risks a blow to the chest for at least 6 weeks.
        • Months later: in one survey, changed chest sensation, tightness and stiff shoulders were common and had fully settled for only 29%. Most were mild, apart from changed sensation, and few people had sought help. If yours hasn't settled, a physiotherapist or osteopath may help with scar mobility, shoulder range and posture.

        After vaginoplasty: dilation, explained

        means gently inserting a smooth, rounded dilator, with plenty of lubricant, to keep the new vagina open while it heals. It begins once the surgical packing comes out, usually about three times a day for the first three months, then less often. Your surgeon will give you a schedule and sizes.

        • Relaxing the pelvic floor, the sling of muscle at the base of the pelvis, makes dilation easier. A pelvic floor physiotherapist can teach you how.
        • Some spotting and discharge are expected early on. Tell your team about bleeding that is heavy, pain that's getting worse, or a dilator that won't go in as far as before.
        • Gas or faeces coming from the vagina needs prompt review: it can be a sign of a fistula.

        Life and people

        For family and supporters

        When someone you love tells you they're trans, you may feel many things at once. This section is written for you. The most important thing to know is that your support makes a real difference to their health.

        Why your support matters

        Support and depression

        At a UK gender clinic, people with more social support had a bigger fall in depression after starting hormones.

        Support and safety

        In a Canadian survey, social support and less transphobia were each linked with a much lower risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts.

        Names matter

        In a US study of young people, being able to use their chosen name in more places was linked with less depression. It's likely to matter at any age.

        What helps

        1. Use their name and pronouns, including when they're not in the room. Practising in private, out loud, helps it become natural.
        2. If you slip, correct it briefly and move on. "Sorry, she's coming at six" is kinder than a long apology, which can leave them comforting you.
        3. Ask, don't assume. What would they like you to call them when you talk about the past? Who knows, and who doesn't? What would help right now?
        4. Keep their story theirs. Don't tell others without their permission: being outed can be unsafe as well as hurtful.
        5. Learn from good sources, such as this guide and TransHub, so they don't have to teach you everything.
        6. Stand up for them when others aren't kind, including in your family, at school or at work.
        7. Show up for the practical things, such as appointments if invited, the long waits, and the weeks after surgery when lifting, cooking and company all help.

        These suggestions come from clinicians' and community experience.

        Your own feelings

        It's common for parents, partners and others to feel worry, confusion or a sense of loss, alongside love. Those feelings are real and deserve support, and they're best taken to a friend, a counsellor or a support line rather than to your loved one in the moment. QLife (1800 184 527) supports family members too, and Rainbow Door (1800 729 367) helps Victorian families.

        Questions families often ask

        Is it a phase?And what if they change their mind?

        Adults usually come out after a long time of thinking. In a US study of children and spouses of military members (average age 19), 70% were still filling hormone prescriptions four years after starting (64% of those who started as adults). Some may have continued elsewhere, and the reasons others stopped weren't recorded. Whatever path your loved one takes, being supported now is linked with better wellbeing, and it keeps the door open for honest conversation later.

        Did I cause this?A question many parents carry

        Being trans isn't something you gave or taught them. What you can shape is how supported and safe they feel now.

        Are hormones and surgery safe?Understanding the risks together

        Hormones are prescribed after informed consent and monitored with regular blood tests, and the risks are known and watched for. Surgery carries real risks and real benefits, and regret is uncommon. If your loved one would like, you could read Staying safe on hormones and Surgery: your options together.

        How do I talk about the past?Photos, old names and memories

        Ask them. Many people prefer their current name and pronouns even when talking about their childhood, and some are happy for old photos to stay up while others aren't. Their answer may change over time.

        I'm their partner. What now?When your relationship changes too

        A partner's transition can raise questions about your own identity, your sex life and how others see you as a couple. You're allowed to have those questions. A counsellor experienced with gender diversity can help you both, and QLife supports partners too.

        For health professionals

        For clinicians

        A practical summary for clinicians, especially in hands-on and musculoskeletal care: affirming practice, what hormones and surgery change in the body you're treating, and the red flags worth screening for.

        Why it matters

        Discrimination keeps people away

        In a national survey of 928 trans Australians, 26% had experienced discrimination in health care, and better clinician training was their top priority.

        Safety changes what people tell you

        Of people who bound, 89% had symptoms but only 15% had sought care related to binding; feeling safe to raise binding with a clinician went with about twice the odds of seeking care.

        Names are clinical care

        Australian guidance asks that names and pronouns be used in consultations and records.

        Affirming practice in a hands-on clinic

        1. Forms and records. Ask for name, pronouns and gender separately from legal and Medicare details, and make sure the name used is the one on screen at reception.
        2. Treat the presenting problem. A sore knee is usually just a sore knee. Ask about gender, hormones or surgery only when it's clinically relevant, and say why you're asking.
        3. Use the person's words for their body, and ask what they prefer if you're unsure. "Chest" rather than "breasts" is often more comfortable for trans men and non-binary people.
        4. Consent before every region, especially the chest, abdomen, pelvis, groin and inner thigh. Explain why, offer to work over clothing or a gown, and make it easy to say stop.
        5. Binders and packers: people may prefer to keep them on. Ask, rather than assuming they'll be removed, and plan the examination around it where you can.
        6. If you get it wrong, correct yourself briefly, thank them, and carry on.

        How we know

        Evidence library

        Every claim in Aster links to its sources. Here is how they were chosen and graded, what the evidence does not support, and the full list, with who each study included.

        How claims are graded

        Strong

        Consistent results from several good , or from very large, well-run studies. Unlikely to change.

        Moderate

        Good evidence with limits: a single or open-label trial, or consistent that follow people over time and show links rather than proof of cause. Could change.

        Weak

        Early, small or indirect evidence: surveys at one point in time (), case series and reviews that pool them, or studies in a different group.

        Not supported

        Tested and not shown, or a claim that didn't hold up when it was checked.

        Guidelines, laws and service pages aren't graded; they're shown as sources. Guideline timings, such as the Endocrine Society's estimates of when changes happen, are shown without a grade too. "Clinical consensus" marks practical advice from clinicians' and community experience where no study exists.

        How the sources were chosen

        • Australian standards come first: the AusPATH Standards of Care (2025) and the Australian position statement on hormone therapy, alongside the international WPATH Standards of Care 8 and the Endocrine Society guideline.
        • Studies were found in PubMed. Every PubMed record, its and DOI was retrieved and checked on 21 November 2026, rather than typed from memory.
        • Each source says who was studied, so you can see when a finding comes from a different group, such as young people or one country, and is being extrapolated.
        • Phone numbers, laws and government services were checked on the services' own websites on the same day.
        • A separate reviewer, who hadn't seen the app being written, then checked every claim against its source. Their 42 findings, two of them about safety advice, were all corrected before release.

        The limits of this evidence

        Most research on gender-affirming care follows groups of people over time rather than randomly assigning treatments, because withholding wanted care is rarely ethical or acceptable. Many studies are small, come from a few specialist clinics in Europe and North America, include few non-binary people, and lose touch with some participants over the years. These limits are why many grades here are moderate or weak. They don't mean care doesn't work; they mean the size of effects is less certain.

        What the evidence doesn't support

        • That pushing hormone levels above the usual target range brings bigger or faster changes. Within usual ranges, blood estradiol levels didn't predict breast growth, and more cyproterone didn't lower testosterone further than a low dose.
        • That testosterone makes people aggressive.
        • That testosterone or estradiol works as contraception.
        • That any binding method is free of side effects. Every method studied, including purpose-made binders, was linked with symptoms.
        • That most people regret surgery: regret was about 1%.
        • The original claim from a Swedish registry study that surgery reduced later mental health treatment; it was corrected after statistical review.
        • That hormones change height, hands, feet or the shape of the skeleton in adults, or that estradiol raises the voice.

        Who does what

        GP (family doctor)Often the first step, and the long-term anchor

        Many GPs start and monitor hormones using informed consent, organise blood tests, screening and referrals, and look after the rest of your health.

        EndocrinologistHormone specialist

        A specialist in hormones, for more complex situations, and for the one-off second opinion needed for PBS-subsidised testosterone (a urologist or sexual health physician can also give this).

        Psychologist or counsellorSupport, not gatekeeping

        For support with dysphoria, anxiety, depression, relationships or the stress of prejudice, and for help thinking through decisions. Your GP can refer you for Medicare-subsidised sessions.

        Speech pathologistVoice training

        Helps you find a voice that feels like yours, safely, and helps with voice strain on testosterone.

        Physiotherapist or osteopathMuscles, joints, posture and recovery

        For pain from binding, recovering movement after top surgery, training safely as strength changes, and hypermobile joints. Pelvic health physiotherapists help before and after genital surgery and with pelvic pain.

        Fertility clinicStoring sperm or eggs

        Talk about storing sperm or eggs before starting hormones or before surgery that removes the testes or ovaries.

        Peer supportPeople who've been there

        Other trans people can offer understanding no clinician can, and in a US survey, support from them softened the link between stigma and distress.
