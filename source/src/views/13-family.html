Life and people

For family and supporters

When someone you love tells you they're trans, you may feel many things at once. This section is written for you. The most important thing to know is that your support makes a real difference to their health.

Why your support matters

Support and depression

At a UK gender clinic, people with more social support had a bigger fall in depression after starting hormones.

Support and safety

In a Canadian survey, social support and less transphobia were each linked with a much lower risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts.

Names matter

In a US study of young people, being able to use their chosen name in more places was linked with less depression. It's likely to matter at any age.

What helps

  1. Use their name and pronouns, including when they're not in the room. Practising in private, out loud, helps it become natural.
  2. If you slip, correct it briefly and move on. "Sorry, she's coming at six" is kinder than a long apology, which can leave them comforting you.
  3. Ask, don't assume. What would they like you to call them when you talk about the past? Who knows, and who doesn't? What would help right now?
  4. Keep their story theirs. Don't tell others without their permission: being outed can be unsafe as well as hurtful.
  5. Learn from good sources, such as this guide and TransHub, so they don't have to teach you everything.
  6. Stand up for them when others aren't kind, including in your family, at school or at work.
  7. Show up for the practical things, such as appointments if invited, the long waits, and the weeks after surgery when lifting, cooking and company all help.

These suggestions come from clinicians' and community experience.

Your own feelings

It's common for parents, partners and others to feel worry, confusion or a sense of loss, alongside love. Those feelings are real and deserve support, and they're best taken to a friend, a counsellor or a support line rather than to your loved one in the moment. QLife (1800 184 527) supports family members too, and Rainbow Door (1800 729 367) helps Victorian families.

Questions families often ask

Is it a phase?And what if they change their mind?

Adults usually come out after a long time of thinking. In a US study of children and spouses of military members (average age 19), 70% were still filling hormone prescriptions four years after starting (64% of those who started as adults). Some may have continued elsewhere, and the reasons others stopped weren't recorded. Whatever path your loved one takes, being supported now is linked with better wellbeing, and it keeps the door open for honest conversation later.

Did I cause this?A question many parents carry

Being trans isn't something you gave or taught them. What you can shape is how supported and safe they feel now.

Are hormones and surgery safe?Understanding the risks together

Hormones are prescribed after informed consent and monitored with regular blood tests, and the risks are known and watched for. Surgery carries real risks and real benefits, and regret is uncommon. If your loved one would like, you could read Staying safe on hormones and Surgery: your options together.

How do I talk about the past?Photos, old names and memories

Ask them. Many people prefer their current name and pronouns even when talking about their childhood, and some are happy for old photos to stay up while others aren't. Their answer may change over time.

I'm their partner. What now?When your relationship changes too

A partner's transition can raise questions about your own identity, your sex life and how others see you as a couple. You're allowed to have those questions. A counsellor experienced with gender diversity can help you both, and QLife supports partners too.
