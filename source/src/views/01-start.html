About this guide

There is no single right way to be trans

Start with three things worth knowing, then the words this guide uses, how it keeps your reading private, and how to read its evidence labels.

Three things to know first

  1. Transition is yours to shape

    Transition, also called affirmation, means the steps you take to live as your gender. They can be social (your name, pronouns, clothes), medical (hormones, surgery) or legal (your documents). Some people want all of these, some want a few, and some want none. "Full transition" is whatever is complete for you.

  2. Hormones work slowly, and differently for everyone

    Most changes from gender-affirming hormones begin within months and keep developing for two to five years. The usual timings are doctors' estimates from clinical observation, and real bodies vary widely.

  3. Care and connection both matter

    In a Melbourne , starting testosterone straight away eased gender dysphoria and depression within three months compared with waiting. Support from other people also matters: social support and being affirmed are linked with better mental health.

Words we use

A few words, explained

People describe themselves in many ways, and the words keep changing. Use the ones that feel right to you. These are the ones this guide uses.

Trans and gender diverse

Trans (transgender) describes people whose gender identity, their inner sense of being a woman, a man, both, neither or something else, differs from the sex they were assigned (recorded) at birth. Gender diverse widens this to include everyone whose gender doesn't fit those two boxes.

Non-binary

A gender that isn't only woman or only man. Some non-binary people want hormones or surgery, some want a few changes, and many want none.

Gender dysphoria and euphoria

Dysphoria is the distress that can come from a mismatch between your gender and your body or how others see you. Euphoria is its joyful counterpart: the sense of rightness when something fits.

Feminising and masculinising

Feminising hormone therapy is based on estradiol, the main oestrogen (a female-typical sex hormone). Masculinising therapy is based on testosterone, the main androgen (a male-typical sex hormone). Together they're called gender-affirming hormone therapy.

How to read the evidence labels. Claims carry a grade and their sources. Tap a source to see who was studied and what was found. Much of the research in this field follows groups of people over time rather than testing treatments in trials, and the grades say so honestly.

Strong: consistent results from trials or very large studies. Moderate: good evidence with limits. Weak: early, small or indirect evidence. Not supported: tested and not shown, or a claim that didn't hold up. Clinical consensus: practical advice from experience where no study exists. More about the grades.

If you're under 18. This guide is written for adults. Care for young people follows separate guidelines and services. In Australia, new national guidelines for under-18s are being developed by the National Health and Medical Research Council, and draft advice on puberty blockers is open for public comment from 16 November 2026 to 18 January 2027. In Victoria, the Royal Children's Hospital Gender Service sees young people under 16, and the Monash Health Gender Clinic accepts referrals from 16. Minus18, Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800) and QLife are there for you now.
