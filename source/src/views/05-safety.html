Hormones

Staying safe on hormones

With regular checks, gender-affirming hormones are effective and, in the short term, relatively safe. Long-term research is still growing, so the checks matter. Here is what's monitored, the risks worth knowing, and the signs that need help quickly.

Signs that need help

Call 000 now

  • Chest pain or pressure, or sudden breathlessness
  • Coughing up blood
  • A face that droops, an arm that's weak, or speech that's slurred (signs of stroke)
  • A sudden, severe headache, especially with changes in vision or weakness
  • Sudden or severe pain in the belly, pelvis, groin or a testis, especially if you could be pregnant
  • Very heavy bleeding with dizziness or fainting

These can be signs of a blood clot in the lung, a stroke, or another emergency such as a pregnancy outside the womb or a twisted testis.

See a doctor straight away

  • Pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one calf or leg, which can be a clot in a deep vein
  • New pain or cramping low in the pelvis, especially if you could be pregnant
  • A headache that won't go away, with blurred vision
  • Very heavy bleeding

Your GP or an after-hours clinic, the same day. If it's severe or getting worse, call 000.

Your regular checks

In Australia, reviews and blood tests are usually every 3 to 4 months in the first year, then once a year once things are stable. Levels are used as safety guides rather than strict targets, because how you feel and change matters too.

The numbers your doctor watchesFor the curious; your doctor interprets them with you

On estradiol: AusPATH recommends keeping estradiol above 250 pmol/L, and notes bone loss is likely below about 182 pmol/L; the 2019 Australian position statement suggested 250 to 600 pmol/L. Testosterone below 2 nmol/L counts as fully suppressed.

On testosterone: the aim is usually the lower part of the typical male range, measured just before the next dose: about 10 to 20 nmol/L in the AusPATH 2025 standards (the 2019 position statement suggested 10 to 15), with haematocrit below 0.50.

Other guidelines use slightly different ranges, and lower-dose plans have different aims. Your own levels make sense only alongside how you're feeling and changing.

Risks worth knowing

Blood clots

The main risk to know

In a large US health-plan study, transfeminine people had about 4 extra clots per 1,000 people over 2 years, and about 17 extra per 1,000 over 8 years, compared with cisgender men; among those who started hormones during the study, the gap was larger and strokes were also more common. Across 18 studies about 2% had a clot, more often with older age and longer use. Smoking, being over 45, obesity and clotting conditions raise the risk, which is why patches or gel may be suggested.

Breast and prostate

Screening for the body you have

Breast cancer was more common in trans women than in cisgender men, but less common than in cisgender women. Prostate cancer was about one-fifth as common as in cisgender men, and the prostate stays after vaginoplasty.

Day to day

Common side effects

Breast tenderness, tiredness, mood changes, and sometimes painful erections. Spironolactone can make you pass more urine and raise potassium.

Thicker blood

Red cells rise

Red cells rise most in the first year, but high levels can first appear years later. In a study that followed 1,073 trans men for up to 20 years, the chance of developing a haematocrit above 0.50 () was about 1 in 10 after one year and nearly 4 in 10 after ten. Smoking roughly doubled the risk; long-acting injections, higher body weight, older age at starting and some lung conditions also raised it. Keep up blood tests for as long as you take testosterone; stopping smoking or switching to gel can help.

Skin and hair

Acne, then hair

Acne usually peaks around 6 months and settles for most people. After about 10 years, about a third had mild and a third moderate to severe male-pattern hair loss.

Heart and sleep

Blood pressure, cholesterol, snoring

Testosterone can raise blood pressure and cholesterol, and can bring on or worsen sleep apnoea (breathing pauses during sleep). Research on heart attack and stroke with testosterone is still mixed.

Pelvis

Dryness and pain

Vaginal dryness is common, and so is new pelvic pain: more than 70% report it in surveys. It's often cramping and low in the pelvis, starting around a year in, and it deserves a check rather than putting up with it. Testosterone also harms a developing baby, so contraception matters.

Cancer screening in Australia

Screening follows the body parts you have, not the gender on your Medicare record. If you've updated your gender with Medicare, reminders may change, so tell your GP what screening you need.

Three things that lower risk

Stopping smoking

Smoking raises the risk of clots on estradiol and roughly doubles the risk of thick blood on testosterone. Quitline (13 7848) is free, and LGBTIQA+ peers work in the service.

Keeping your appointments

Most problems, such as a rising red-cell count or potassium, show up on blood tests before you feel them.

Hormones with a prescription

Hormones bought online without a prescription may not contain what the label says, and without blood tests problems go unnoticed. If cost or waiting is the barrier, an informed-consent GP may be quicker than you think. If you already use them, tell your doctor: you deserve safe care, not judgement.
