Mind

Mind and emotions

Transition can bring relief and joy, and hard days too. Hormones change how feelings feel for some people, and how others treat you shapes your mental health as much as anything happening in your body. Here is what to expect, and what helps.

How hormones can feel

On estradiol

Softer edges

Many people describe feeling calmer, with emotions closer to the surface, so tears may come more easily. Mood on estradiol is little studied, so this comes mostly from clinical experience.

Sex drive and spontaneous erections usually lessen in the first three months. By three years, desire for sex with a partner was, on average, higher than before starting.

On testosterone

A stronger drive, not more anger

Sex drive often rises in the first months, which can feel intense. By three years, desire for sex with a partner was, on average, back near where it started, while desire for solo sex stayed higher.

In a UK study that followed people for a year, testosterone did not increase self-reported aggression. Aggression went with anxiety, not with hormone levels.

Either way

Waiting is hard too

Waiting for appointments, waiting for changes and seeing them begin can all stir up strong feelings. Low mood that lasts more than two weeks, or any thoughts of harming yourself, are worth raising with your GP straight away.

What studies show about mental health

3 mo

In a Melbourne trial with 64 adults, starting testosterone straight away eased gender dysphoria, depression and suicidal thoughts within three months, compared with waiting.

18 mo

At a UK gender clinic, depression fell clearly after 18 months on hormones, while anxiety did not change significantly. People with more social support improved more.

20

studies in one review linked hormones with better quality of life and less depression and anxiety, though the authors rated their certainty as limited because of how the studies were designed.

Hormones ease gender distress for most people, but they aren't a cure for everything. Many people still need support for anxiety, past trauma or ordinary life stress. Needing that support doesn't mean transition isn't working.

Dysphoria and euphoria

When dysphoria is loud

can come in waves: around mirrors, photos, certain clothes, being misgendered, or for no clear reason. Some things people find help on hard days:

  • Clothes, binding or padding that feel right, worn safely
  • Less time with mirrors or photos for a while
  • A message to someone who sees you as you are
  • Something absorbing: a walk, music, a task for your hands
  • Reminding yourself the feeling will pass, even if the reason doesn't

Noticing euphoria

is the joyful sense of rightness when something fits. People describe it coming from within, from their body or clothes, or from being seen correctly by others.

Euphoria is worth noticing, not just dysphoria: it shows you what to move toward. You can record those moments in My milestones.

The stress of prejudice

Being trans is not a mental illness. Trans people's higher rates of anxiety and depression are strongly linked to how they're treated. This is called : ongoing, extra stress from prejudice, piled on top of everyday life. It comes from outside, such as rejection, discrimination or violence, and then works its way inside, as expecting rejection, hiding, or turning others' prejudice on yourself (). Surveys show the link but can't show how much of the difference it explains. Support, community and pride are linked with softer effects.

What protects your mental health

People who back you

Support from family, friends and partners was linked with a bigger fall in depression on hormones and, in a Canadian survey, with a much lower risk of suicidal thoughts.

Your name, used

In a US study of young people, each extra place they could use their chosen name (home, school, work, friends) was linked with less depression and fewer suicidal thoughts. It's likely to matter for adults too, though adults weren't studied.

Documents that match

People whose identity documents all showed their name and gender had about a third less serious psychological distress than those with none that matched. Changing your documents.

Other trans people

In a US survey, support from other trans people softened the link between stigma and distress. Peer groups, online communities and trans-led services can all offer this.

Care when you need it

Getting wanted medical care, when people needed it, was linked with much lower risk of suicidal thoughts in a Canadian survey, and starting sooner helped in the Melbourne trial.

Pride

Connection to community and pride in who you are are named as sources of resilience in the gender minority stress model.

Autism, ADHD and neurodiversity

Trans and gender diverse people are more likely than others to be autistic or to have ADHD or another difference. In the Australian survey, 15% reported autism and 11% ADHD. Being neurodivergent doesn't make your gender less real. It can help to tell your clinicians what makes appointments easier for you, such as written information, extra time or a quiet waiting space.

Body image and eating

Feeling unhappy with your body is at the heart of the distress many trans people feel, and it may put some at risk of disordered eating. Gender-affirming care tended to improve how people felt about their bodies.

If food, weight or exercise is starting to feel like a way to control your shape, please talk to your GP, or call the Butterfly National Helpline on 1800 33 4673 (8 am to midnight, every day). Support works, and you don't need to be very unwell to ask.

Finding the right counsellor

Not a gatekeeper

Adults in Australia don't need a mental health assessment to start hormones. A counsellor or psychologist is there because you want support, or if other concerns need care first.

Look for experience

Ask whether they've worked with trans and gender diverse people, and how. A good counsellor follows your lead rather than steering you toward or away from transition. The AusPATH directory lists affirming clinicians; your GP can refer you for Medicare-subsidised psychology sessions.

Conversion practices are banned

In Victoria it's against the law to try to change or suppress someone's gender identity. If it has happened to you, you can report it to the Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission.

Doubts, pausing and changing course

Doubt is human. Doubting a step, such as a dose or an operation, isn't the same as doubting who you are, and both are fine to talk through with your clinicians.

Most people continue

In a US study of children and spouses of military members (average age 19), 70% were still filling hormone prescriptions four years after starting (64% of those who started as adults). Some may have continued outside that health system. Why others stopped wasn't recorded, and stopping isn't the same as regret.

Many reasons for stopping

In a large US survey, 13% had ever stopped or gone back, even briefly, and most of them named outside pressures such as family or stigma. In a smaller survey of 100 people who detransitioned, reasons included becoming more comfortable with their birth sex, health concerns, and coming to see their distress as caused by something else.

Care either way

If you're thinking about stopping or changing course, tell your doctor: some changes ease and some don't, and if your testes or ovaries have been removed you'll still need hormones to protect your bones. You deserve the same respect whichever way you go.
