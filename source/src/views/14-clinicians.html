For health professionals

For clinicians

A practical summary for clinicians, especially in hands-on and musculoskeletal care: affirming practice, what hormones and surgery change in the body you're treating, and the red flags worth screening for.

Why it matters

Discrimination keeps people away

In a national survey of 928 trans Australians, 26% had experienced discrimination in health care, and better clinician training was their top priority.

Safety changes what people tell you

Of people who bound, 89% had symptoms but only 15% had sought care related to binding; feeling safe to raise binding with a clinician went with about twice the odds of seeking care.

Names are clinical care

Australian guidance asks that names and pronouns be used in consultations and records.

Affirming practice in a hands-on clinic

  1. Forms and records. Ask for name, pronouns and gender separately from legal and Medicare details, and make sure the name used is the one on screen at reception.
  2. Treat the presenting problem. A sore knee is usually just a sore knee. Ask about gender, hormones or surgery only when it's clinically relevant, and say why you're asking.
  3. Use the person's words for their body, and ask what they prefer if you're unsure. "Chest" rather than "breasts" is often more comfortable for trans men and non-binary people.
  4. Consent before every region, especially the chest, abdomen, pelvis, groin and inner thigh. Explain why, offer to work over clothing or a gown, and make it easy to say stop.
  5. Binders and packers: people may prefer to keep them on. Ask, rather than assuming they'll be removed, and plan the examination around it where you can.
  6. If you get it wrong, correct yourself briefly, thank them, and carry on.

What changes in the body you're treating

Musculoskeletal considerations
AreaWhat to knowEvidence
Tendons on testosteroneMuscle strength rises within months (thigh muscle about +15% in a year) while adapt more slowly, so loading should progress gradually. The tendon finding comes from adolescent athletes, so this is an extrapolation.
Strength on estradiolHaemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in blood, falls to the female range within about 4 months; strength and lean mass decline over the first year but stay above cisgender women's averages at 3 years.
BoneBone density rises in the first year of hormones. Trans women may start with lower bone density. Trans women over 50 had more fractures than men their age, similar to women. After the testes or ovaries are removed, hormone adherence protects bone.
HypermobilityHypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and spectrum disorder were diagnosed far more often in trans and gender diverse people in a large records study. Screen for it in recurrent sprains and widespread joint pain.
Chest bindingBack and chest pain, shortness of breath, overheating and skin problems are common, and more frequent with more binding days per week. Rib and thoracic mobility, breathing pattern and posture are worth assessing.
After top surgeryAltered chest sensation (82%), chest tightness (71%) and shoulder stiffness (56%) were common, and only 28% had sought treatment such as physiotherapy. Arms usually stay below shoulder height for about 4 weeks.
Pelvis dysfunction is common before vaginoplasty, and in one program, physiotherapy before and after surgery was linked with fewer problems afterwards. On testosterone, new pelvic pain is very common (self-reported by more than 70%), often cramping and starting around a year in; vaginal dryness and irritation are common too.
VoiceAbout a quarter of people on testosterone in one study had voice problems such as instability or fatigue, and had voice therapy.

Red flags to screen for

Estradiol

Blood clots

Signs of : calf pain or swelling, or breathlessness or chest pain, especially after surgery, a long journey or a period of immobility. Feminising hormones raise the risk: in a large US health-plan study, venous clots were more common than in cisgender men and women, and strokes too among those who started hormones during the study.

Cyproterone

Meningioma

A can show as new or changing headaches, visual change, or new neurological signs in someone who has used cyproterone, especially at higher doses or for years.

Testosterone

Haematocrit

, a above 0.50, had a cumulative probability of about 10% at one year and 38% at ten years on testosterone: the rise is greatest in the first year, but new cases keep appearing. Risk is higher with smoking, long-acting undecanoate injections, higher BMI, older age at starting and lung disease. Haematocrit needs checking for as long as testosterone continues, so ask when bloods were last done.

Tucking

Testicular torsion

Sudden, severe testicular or groin pain in someone who tucks needs emergency assessment.

Working as a team

  • The GP or prescriber manages hormones and blood tests; let them know about red flags or new symptoms.
  • Pelvic health physiotherapists, speech pathologists for voice, and psychologists experienced with gender diversity complement hands-on care.
  • The AusPATH directory lists clinicians who provide gender-affirming care across Australia.
