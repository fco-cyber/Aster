Body

Body and movement

Hormones change muscle, fat and bone, and so they change how your body moves, feels and recovers. Knowing what's happening under the skin helps you train well, stay comfortable and avoid injury.

Muscle and strength

On testosterone

Strength comes quickly

In the first year, thigh muscle volume rose by about 15% in a small Swedish study, and grip strength rose by about 6 kg on average in a larger European one. Men's-standard fitness results were usually matched within a year.

For training: muscles get stronger faster than adapt, especially while hormones are rising. Building load gradually over months, particularly for running, jumping and heavy lifting, gives tendons time to catch up. This comes from studies of teenagers going through puberty, so it's an educated extrapolation.

On estradiol

Muscle eases, slowly

Thigh muscle volume fell by about 5% in a year while strength was largely kept, and grip strength fell by about 2 kg. Whether strength keeps falling after the first year isn't clear, but on average it stays above cisgender women's after three years.

For training: haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, falls to the usual female range within about four months, so hard efforts may feel tougher at first. Keep strength training: it protects muscle and bone. If hard sessions feel tougher, it's fine to lower the load for a while.

Fat and body shape

Hormones move fat rather than simply adding or removing it. On estradiol, fat builds mostly on the hips and thighs, and the hip measurement grew by about 3 cm in a year. On testosterone, fat leaves the hips and thighs while the waist stays about the same, so the shape becomes straighter. These changes keep developing for two to five years.

Changes in body shape can bring up strong feelings about food and weight. If eating is becoming a way to control your shape, please talk to your GP; body dissatisfaction is a known risk for disordered eating in trans people, and support helps.

Bones

Hormones protect bone

In the first year of hormones, rose: in trans women at the spine (about 4%) and hip, and in trans men at the hip. Sex hormones are what keep bones strong, so the risk comes from long stretches without them.

A gap before starting

Before hormones, trans women in one small study had lower bone density, less muscle and lower vitamin D than other men, which the authors linked to being less active. Being active supports bone and muscle whenever you start.

Later in life

Trans women over 50 had more fractures than men their age, about the same as women their age. Trans men had no higher risk. If your testes or ovaries are removed, keep taking hormones: bone loss is likely when levels stay low.

What helps your bones: strength training at least twice a week and weight-bearing activity such as walking, food rich in calcium, some safe sun for vitamin D, not smoking, and taking your hormones consistently.

Joints, flexibility and hypermobility

In a large set of US health records, conditions were diagnosed far more often in trans and gender diverse people than in cisgender people, whether or not they used hormones. The reason isn't known, and a records study can't show how common it really is, but it's worth knowing.

  • If your joints bend further than most people's and you get frequent sprains, aches or clicking, mention it to your GP, physiotherapist or osteopath.
  • Strength and control exercises usually help hypermobile joints more than extra stretching does.
  • On testosterone, rapid strength gains can tempt you to lift more than your joints and tendons are ready for: progress steadily.

Posture, the chest and the shoulders

Some people who are uncomfortable with their chest round their shoulders and drop their head to hide it, sometimes for years, which can stiffen the upper back and the front of the chest. Binding adds pressure on the ribs and breathing muscles (see Binding and tucking).

After top surgery, muscle and joint problems are common and often missed. In a Canadian survey, most people had altered chest sensation (82%) and chest tightness (71%), and over half had stiff shoulders (56%). Most were mild, apart from changed sensation, but only 29% said they had fully settled, and only 28% had sought treatment such as physiotherapy.

Gentle upper-back mobility, slow deep breathing into the lower ribs, and exercises that strengthen the muscles between the shoulder blades can all help, once any surgical restrictions have ended. A physiotherapist or osteopath can tailor this to you.

Your voice

On testosterone

A deeper voice, for good

Average speaking dropped clearly within three months and kept falling to a year, reaching the typical male range for most. About a quarter had an unstable or tired voice and had voice therapy. The change is permanent.

On estradiol

Training, not hormones

Estradiol doesn't change the voice. Voice training with a speech pathologist raised average pitch by about 31 Hz, with roughly 80 to 85% of people satisfied, and changes in pitch and were mostly kept a year later. Surgery can raise pitch further but can make the voice quieter.

Protect your voice as you train: practise little and often, keep your throat relaxed, and stop if it hurts or goes hoarse. In Victoria, the La Trobe University voice clinic can be reached through the Monash Health Gender Clinic, though waits are long; private speech pathologists also offer gender-affirming voice training.

Moving more, comfortably

In a UK study, trans people were, on average, less active than cisgender people, and those on hormones were more active than those not yet on them. Across studies, the biggest barrier to sport and exercise was not having comfortable, inclusive spaces, such as changing rooms.

The guideline

Australian adults are advised to be active on most days, with muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week and balance and mobility work on 3. Any activity is better than none.

Ways around the barriers

Home or park workouts, walking or running groups, gyms with private changing areas, LGBTIQA+ sports clubs, swimming in a rash vest and board shorts, or quieter session times.

Competitive sport

Rules differ by sport and level, and are changing. Ask your club or the sport's governing body. In Victoria, the Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission has a guideline on including trans and gender diverse people in sport.

The pelvis and pelvic floor

The is the sling of muscle at the base of the pelvis. On testosterone, new pelvic pain is very common, reported by more than 70% in surveys; it's often cramping and low in the pelvis, starting around a year in. It should be checked by a GP or gynaecologist, and a pelvic health physiotherapist may help. Before vaginoplasty, pelvic floor problems are common: in one US program, 42% had them, and people who saw a pelvic floor physiotherapist both before and after surgery had far fewer problems afterwards.
