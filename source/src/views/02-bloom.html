    The seven stages

    Your journey, stage by stage

    Transition isn't a straight line or a race. These seven stages are a map, not a rulebook: you might linger in one, skip another, move back and forth, or stop where you feel at home. Each stage has its own milestones worth marking.

    1. Wondering and exploringAny age, any length of time

      Many people spend months or years wondering about their gender before they say anything. You might try a different name online, notice which clothes or pronouns feel right, or read about other people's stories. Questioning doesn't commit you to anything.

      You might feel
      Curious, relieved, frightened, confused, or all of these in one day. Moments of , a quiet sense of "yes, this fits", are useful signposts.
      What helps
      Talking with other trans people, by phone at QLife or in a peer group such as Transgender Victoria's; a counsellor who works with gender diversity and won't push you either way; noting when you feel most yourself.
      Milestones
      Saying it out loud, even only to yourself. Telling one trusted person. Trying a name or pronouns somewhere safe.
    2. Finding careWeeks to months, depending on waits

      For most adults in Australia the first step is a GP. Many GPs now prescribe hormones, and the Australian Professional Association for Trans Health (AusPATH) lists experienced clinicians. In Victoria there are also specialist services: Equinox, a GP clinic for trans and gender diverse people; the Monash Health Gender Clinic, where waits can be long; and Austin Health's specialist hormone clinic, which works alongside your own doctor.

      Bring
      What you hope for, your questions, your medicines and health history, and a support person if you'd like one.
      Ask
      "Do you prescribe gender-affirming hormones, or can you refer me to someone who does?" and "Do you bulk-bill, and what will it cost?"
      Milestones
      Booking the first appointment. Being called by your name at the clinic for the first time.
    3. Deciding togetherOne to a few appointments

      Adults in Australia usually start hormones through : your doctor makes sure you understand what hormones can and can't do, the risks, which changes are permanent, and how they may affect fertility, and then you decide. A mental health assessment isn't required, but a psychologist or counsellor is involved if you'd like one, or if other health concerns need care first. You'll have baseline blood tests.

      Testosterone subsidised on the needs a one-off second opinion from an endocrinologist, urologist or sexual health physician, which can be by telehealth.

      Think about
      Fertility. Hormones can reduce fertility, possibly for good, so storing sperm or eggs beforehand is worth discussing even if you're unsure you'll ever want children. More on fertility.
      Milestones
      Signing your consent. Your first blood test. Making your fertility decision, whatever it is.
    4. Starting hormonesThe first 3 months

      The first changes are often felt before they're seen. On estradiol, sexual desire and spontaneous erections usually lessen within one to three months, and some people notice tender nipples or breasts. On testosterone, skin often becomes oilier, sex drive often rises, and periods may begin to change. Your first review and blood tests usually come at around three months.

      You might feel
      Excited, impatient, or worried that nothing is happening. Early weeks are normal and slow; the biggest changes come later.
      Milestones
      Your first dose. Your first three-month check.
    5. Visible changeAbout 3 to 12 months

      This is when many people start to see themselves in the mirror. On estradiol, breasts grow (most of the first year's growth happens in the first six months), skin softens and fat begins to move toward the hips. On testosterone, the voice deepens, facial and body hair start to grow, muscle builds, and for most people periods have stopped by 12 months. Reviews continue every three to four months.

      Alongside
      Many people change their name and pronouns at work or study, start voice training, or update documents in this period.
      Milestones
      A voice that feels like yours. Periods stopping. A new name on your Medicare card. A day of not thinking about your gender at all.
    6. Growing into yourselfAbout 1 to 3 years

      Changes continue, more gently. Breast volume keeps increasing slowly for at least three years, and facial hair and body shape keep developing for two to five years. Once things are stable, check-ups usually drop to once a year. If you're considering surgery, this is when many people meet surgeons, and some operations ask for about six months of stable hormones first.

      You might feel
      Calmer, and also more aware of what hormones can't change, such as height or bone structure. Both are normal.
      Milestones
      One year on hormones. A new birth certificate. A first surgical consultation, if that's your path.
    7. Settled, and ageing wellThree years and beyond

      For many people, transition becomes less of a project and more of a life. Care continues: most people keep taking hormones long term, with yearly blood tests and check-ups, attention to bone and heart health, and cancer screening for the body you have now.

      Keep in mind
      If your testes or ovaries are removed, you'll need to keep taking hormones to protect your bones. Health checks and screening.
      Milestones
      Five years. Screening up to date. Helping someone else find their way.
