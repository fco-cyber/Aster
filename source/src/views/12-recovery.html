Surgery: recovery

What recovery usually looks like, stage by stage, for the most common operations, and the signs that need help. These timings come from surgical teams' published instructions; your own surgeon's plan always comes first.

    Warning signs after any operation

    Call 000 now

    • Chest pain or sudden breathlessness
    • Coughing up blood
    • Fainting, or feeling very unwell very quickly
    • Heavy bleeding that won't stop with firm pressure

    These can be signs of a blood clot in the lung or serious bleeding.

    Call your surgical team straight away

    • Swelling on one side that grows quickly or feels tight
    • Not being able to pass urine
    • A flap, nipple graft or new penis that changes colour (pale, blue or dark) or feels cold
    • Pain, swelling or warmth in one calf
    • A temperature of 38 °C or more, or shivers
    • Redness spreading from the wound, or pus or a bad smell

    If you can't reach them, or it's after hours, go to an emergency department and take your surgery details.

    Usual, and worth mentioning

    • Bruising and swelling that slowly ease
    • Numbness or tingling near the scars
    • Tiredness for several weeks
    • Feeling flat or tearful

    Raise these at your follow-up visit.

    Recovering well

    Walk early, little and often

    Short, gentle walks from the first days, as your team allows, help prevent blood clots and stiffness. Slow, deep breaths a few times an hour help your lungs.

    Stay ahead of pain

    Take pain relief as prescribed rather than waiting for pain to build. Strong pain medicines often cause constipation, so drink water and ask about a gentle laxative.

    Scars mature slowly

    Scars are usually red and firm at first and fade over 12 to 18 months. Once the wound has fully healed, your team may suggest massage, silicone gel or sheets, and protection from the sun.

    Feelings after surgery

    Many people feel flat, tearful or unsettled in the weeks after a big operation, even one they wanted very much: tiredness, pain and being stuck at home all play a part. It usually passes. If it doesn't, or if you feel unsafe, tell your GP or team.

    Moving again after top surgery

    • Until about 4 weeks: gentle everyday arm movement, keeping your elbows below shoulder height.
    • Until 4 to 6 weeks: nothing heavier than about 2 kg, and no strenuous exercise.
    • From about 4 weeks: slow, comfortable reaching and gentle shoulder and upper-back mobility, as your surgeon allows. Avoid anything that risks a blow to the chest for at least 6 weeks.
    • Months later: in one survey, changed chest sensation, tightness and stiff shoulders were common and had fully settled for only 29%. Most were mild, apart from changed sensation, and few people had sought help. If yours hasn't settled, a physiotherapist or osteopath may help with scar mobility, shoulder range and posture.

    After vaginoplasty: dilation, explained

    means gently inserting a smooth, rounded dilator, with plenty of lubricant, to keep the new vagina open while it heals. It begins once the surgical packing comes out, usually about three times a day for the first three months, then less often. Your surgeon will give you a schedule and sizes.

    • Relaxing the pelvic floor, the sling of muscle at the base of the pelvis, makes dilation easier. A pelvic floor physiotherapist can teach you how.
    • Some spotting and discharge are expected early on. Tell your team about bleeding that is heavy, pain that's getting worse, or a dilator that won't go in as far as before.
    • Gas or faeces coming from the vagina needs prompt review: it can be a sign of a fistula.
