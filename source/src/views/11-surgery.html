Surgery

Surgery: your options

Surgery is one option among many, not a final step everyone takes. Some people want several operations, some one, and many none. Here are the main options, what results are really like, and how to prepare.

You don't need surgery to be who you are. In Victoria you can change the sex on your birth certificate without any surgery or medical evidence, and "full transition" is whatever is complete for you.

What surgeons usually look for

International standards suggest these for adults having gender-affirming surgery. Your surgeon will tell you what assessments or letters they need.

  • Your gender incongruence is marked and has lasted over time.
  • Other things that could explain the distress have been considered.
  • You can give informed consent, and you understand how the operation affects fertility.
  • Other physical or mental health conditions that could affect surgery or recovery have been assessed and are being cared for.
  • For some operations, about six months of stable hormone treatment first, unless hormones aren't wanted or aren't medically suitable.

The main operations

Numbers come from reviews that pooled many surgical studies. Results vary between techniques and surgeons, so ask your surgeon for their own. Recovery week by week is in Surgery: recovery.

Chest reconstruction (top surgery)Removes breast tissue and shapes a flatter chest

removes most breast tissue and reshapes the chest. For larger chests the usual method is a double incision, with the nipples resized and placed back as skin grafts; smaller chests can sometimes be done through a small cut around the nipple.

Results
Across 8 studies, 6% of operated sides needed an urgent return to theatre for bleeding under the skin (), and about a quarter of sides (26.5%) needed a further operation later. Double incision with nipple grafts had fewer urgent returns than two other methods.
Afterwards
Most people return to work in 4 to 6 weeks. Changed chest sensation, tightness and stiff shoulders are common, mostly mild apart from changed sensation, and physiotherapy may help.
Keep in mind
There's no set screening after top surgery. Be chest aware: get to know how your chest looks and feels, see your GP about any changes, and ask your surgeon whether any breast tissue was left.
Breast augmentationImplants or fat transfer to add breast size

Breast growth on estradiol continues slowly for at least three years, and most people's breasts stay smaller than an A cup, so some people choose augmentation once their own growth has slowed.

Ask about
Implant type and size, where the cut will be, scar care, and how often implants need checking or replacing over the years. If you go for breast screening, tell the staff you have implants.
Facial surgeryForehead, nose, jaw, chin and the Adam's apple

is a group of operations that soften features hormones can't change, because bone keeps its adult shape. Reducing the Adam's apple is often called a tracheal shave. People choose the parts that matter most to them.

Ask about
Which procedures, the surgeon's own results and photos, swelling (which can take months to settle fully), numbness and costs.
Voice surgeryRaising pitch by changing the vocal folds

Operations such as can raise pitch more than voice training alone, but carry risks such as a quieter or rougher voice. Many people try voice training first, and keep it up after surgery.

Removal of the testes (orchidectomy)A shorter operation, alone or before vaginoplasty

An removes the testes, the main source of testosterone. Afterwards anti-androgens are no longer needed, but you'll still need estradiol to protect your bones. It ends sperm production for good, so think about storing sperm first.

Regret
In 6,793 people seen at the Amsterdam clinic over 43 years, 0.6% of trans women who had their testes removed were identified as regretting it.
Vaginoplasty and vulvoplastyCreating a vulva, with or without a vaginal canal

creates a vulva (clitoris, labia) and a vaginal canal, most often using penile and scrotal skin (penile inversion), or sometimes a section of bowel. creates the vulva without a canal, so there's less recovery and no dilation. The prostate stays in place.

Results
Across 57 studies of 4,680 people, 91% were satisfied and 2% expressed regret. Narrowing of the vagina (stenosis) happened in 11%, an abnormal opening into the bowel or bladder () in 1%, tissue loss in 4% and prolapse in 3%. Average depth was 9.4 cm with penile inversion and 15.3 cm with bowel.
Dilation
keeps the new canal open: about three times a day for the first three months, then less often, on a schedule your surgeon sets.
Keep in mind
You'll still have a prostate, so it still needs checking as you age.
Hysterectomy, with or without removal of the ovariesRemoving the uterus, and often the cervix and ovaries

A removes the uterus, and often the cervix. The ovaries may be removed too. It can be done through small cuts in the belly (keyhole surgery), through the vagina, or through a larger cut.

Afterwards
Usually 2 to 5 days in hospital, and up to 5 to 7 weeks before usual activities. If your ovaries are removed, you'll need to keep taking testosterone to protect your bones.
Keep in mind
Ask whether you still need cervical screening: it depends on whether the cervix was removed. Removing the ovaries ends your own egg supply, so think about storing eggs first. In the Amsterdam study, 0.3% of trans men who had their ovaries removed were identified as regretting it.
MetoidioplastyA small penis made from the testosterone-enlarged clitoris

frees the clitoris, which has grown on testosterone, to form a small penis. It can include lengthening the urethra (the tube that carries urine) so you can pass urine standing, and creating a scrotum.

Results
Across 7 studies of 403 people, most could pass urine standing. A fistula (a leak from a new opening) happened in 5 to 37% and narrowing of the urethra () in 2 to 35%, depending on the technique.
PhalloplastyA larger penis built from a skin flap, in stages

builds a penis from a flap of skin and tissue, most often from the forearm or thigh (the ). It usually happens in several stages over a year or more, with an implant for erections placed around 9 months or later after the penis is made.

Results
Across 39 studies of 1,731 people, most could feel touch (94%) and pass urine standing (92%), where this was reported. Complications were common: about three-quarters (76.5%) had at least one, mostly in the urethra, with a fistula in 34% and a stricture in 25%.
Donor site
Can bring scarring, wound breakdown, pain, and reduced feeling or movement.

Surgery and wellbeing: what studies show

~1%

expressed regret across 27 studies of 7,928 people who had gender-affirming surgery, though definitions varied and follow-up was often incomplete.

2 yrs+

after surgery, people in a large US survey had lower psychological distress and fewer suicidal thoughts than people who wanted surgery but hadn't had it. A one-off survey can't prove cause.

Long-term studies, and their limitsTwo Swedish studies that are often quoted. Opens only if you choose.

Sweden, 1973 to 2003. After surgery, rates of death, suicidal behaviour and psychiatric admission were higher than in the general population. The study had no comparison group of trans people who didn't have surgery, so it can't say whether surgery helped or not; the authors called for better ongoing care after surgery.

Sweden, national register. A first analysis linked more time since surgery with less mental health treatment. After statistical review, the journal published a correction: compared with people who hadn't had surgery, there was no advantage for later mental health care.

What both show clearly is that care shouldn't stop at the operating theatre. Support for mental health, and for the stresses of prejudice, matters for years afterwards. Mind and emotions.

Preparing well

  1. Stop smoking and vaping, ideally months ahead. Nicotine narrows blood vessels and slows healing, and many surgeons won't operate on people who smoke. Quitline: 13 7848.
  2. Start hair removal early if your operation needs it; it can take many months.
  3. See a pelvic floor physiotherapist before and after vaginoplasty if you can: in one US program, people seen both before and after had far fewer pelvic floor problems afterwards (28% compared with 86%).
  4. Ask about your hormones around surgery. Some surgeons pause estradiol to lower clot risk; in one US centre, continuing it through vaginoplasty wasn't linked with more clots. Follow your own team's plan.
  5. Decide about fertility before any operation that removes the testes, ovaries or uterus.
  6. Line up help at home for the first weeks: lifting, shopping, driving, and company. Plan time off work, using the recovery times in the next section.
  7. Plan the costs and follow-up. Ask about the full cost, including hospital and anaesthetist fees, what Medicare and private health insurance cover, and any public options in your state. If you go interstate or overseas, plan who will look after you when you're home.
Questions to ask your surgeonTake these to your consultation
  • How many of these operations do you do each year, and what are your own complication and revision rates?
  • Which technique do you recommend for me, and why?
  • What do I need to do beforehand: hormones, hair removal, weight, smoking?
  • How long will I be in hospital, off work, and unable to drive, lift, exercise or have sex?
  • Who do I call after hours if something seems wrong?
  • What does follow-up look like, and for how long?
  • What is the total cost, and what if I need a revision?
