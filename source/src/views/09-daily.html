Life and people

Daily life and habits

The everyday side of care: taking your hormones well, sex and sexual health, skin and hair, sleep and drinking, and travelling with your medicines.

Taking your hormones well

Patches

A new spot each time

Follow the leaflet for where and how often to change them. Moving each new patch to a different spot is gentler on your skin. If one falls off, ask your pharmacist what to do.

Gels

Keep it to yourself

Testosterone gel can pass to other people through skin contact. Wash your hands after applying, let it dry and cover the area with clothing. Before close skin contact, shower or wash the area with soap and water, and leave at least 6 hours between applying it and intimate contact. Keep children, anyone pregnant or breastfeeding, and pets away from the treated skin; if someone touches it, wash their skin with soap and water. For estradiol gel, ask your pharmacist about the same care.

Injections

Safe and steady

Use a new needle every time, change the spot you inject, and put used needles in a sharps container; your pharmacy or council can tell you where to return it. A practice nurse can teach you, or give the injection for you.

Tablets

Same time, every day

A weekly pill box or a phone reminder helps. If you miss a dose of any hormone, check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist; don't double up unless you've been told to.

Reorder before you run low, because your pharmacy may need to order your medicine in. Keep every blood-test appointment: they're how problems are caught early. Your regular checks.

Sex, intimacy and sexual health

Your sex life may change

On estradiol, desire and spontaneous erections usually lessen within months; on testosterone, desire often rises early. Both tend to settle over the following years. Talking with partners about what feels good now, which may be different, helps.

Dryness on testosterone

Dryness, irritation or soreness of the vagina and vulva on testosterone is very common, estimated at 64 to 78% within five years. Lubricant and moisturisers help, and a doctor can check for infection and discuss a low-dose vaginal estrogen that acts locally. Evidence for treatment is still limited.

Protecting yourself

If you have new partners, regular sexual health checks and condoms protect you. PrEP, a medicine that prevents HIV, is recommended as a daily tablet for trans women and for trans men who have vaginal sex; the on-demand way of taking it hasn't been shown to work for them.

Tell a sexual health clinic about your body, including any surgery, so they offer the right tests. Neither testosterone nor estradiol is contraception: fertility and contraception.

Skin and hair

Testosterone

Acne, then calmer skin

Oilier skin and acne usually peak around six months and settle for most people. A gentle cleanser and non-greasy moisturiser help; if acne is painful, scarring or getting you down, your GP has effective treatments.

Testosterone

Scalp hair

After about 10 years on testosterone, a third had mild and a third moderate to severe male-pattern hair loss. If it bothers you, talk to your GP about the options and how they might affect other changes.

Estradiol

Facial and body hair

Estradiol slows facial and body hair growth but rarely stops it. Laser heats the dark pigment in hair, so it works best on dark hair and usually reduces rather than removes it; electrolysis destroys hair roots one at a time and works on any colour. Both take many sessions.

Before genital surgery

Hair removal first

For vaginoplasty, surgeons usually ask for hair to be cleared from the skin that will line the vagina, often with electrolysis over several months, so start early. Ask your surgeon whether your operation needs this too.

Sleep, food and drink

Sleep

Adults need 7 to 9 hours of good-quality sleep. On testosterone, new or louder snoring, or feeling sleepy in the day, can be a sign of sleep apnoea and is worth mentioning.

Food

Appetite and weight can shift as hormones move fat and muscle around, so the scales don't tell the whole story. Regular, varied meals with enough protein and calcium support your muscles and bones.

Alcohol, smoking and vaping

Australian guidelines advise no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any day. Smoking raises the risk of clots on estradiol and of thick blood on testosterone; Quitline (13 7848) has LGBTIQA+ peers working in the service.

Travelling with your medicines

  1. Get a letter from your doctor naming each medicine and dose and saying it's for your personal use, and keep your prescriptions with you.
  2. Keep medicines in their original packaging with the pharmacy label, and pack them, with any needles, in your hand luggage. Take enough for the whole trip.
  3. Check your destination. Some medicines are restricted overseas, and some countries criminalise LGBTQIA+ people, including laws aimed at people who dress as another gender. Read Smartraveller's advice for each country before you book.
  4. Think about your passport. Australian passports can show M, F or X, but some countries don't recognise X.
