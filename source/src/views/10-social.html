Life and people

Family, friends and the world

Living as yourself happens among other people. Here is help with telling people, relationships, work and study, your rights in Australia, and changing your name and documents.

Telling people

Coming out is yours to pace. There's no deadline and no one you have to tell first. A few things many people find useful:

  1. Start where it feels safest. Often that's one person you expect to be supportive, who can then back you up with others.
  2. Think about safety first if you depend on someone for housing, money or care, or if there's been violence at home. Rainbow Door (1800 729 367) can help you plan, and Safe Steps (1800 015 188) answers day and night.
  3. Choose the moment and the way. Face to face, by phone or in a letter or message are all fine. Writing it down first can help you say what matters most.
  4. Have support lined up afterwards, whatever happens: a friend, a peer group, or QLife on 1800 184 527.
  5. Give people time. First reactions aren't always final ones. You don't owe anyone a debate about who you are.
Ways to start the conversationWords some people have used; make them your own
  • "There's something important about me I'd like you to know, and I'm telling you because you matter to me."
  • "I've known for a while that I'm trans. I'm going to start living as myself, and I'd love you to be part of that."
  • "My name is now Sam, and I use they/them. It might take practice, and that's OK. What matters is that you try."
  • "You don't have to understand everything today. I'm happy to answer questions, and there's a guide for families I can share."

The section for family and supporters is written so you can share it.

Partners and relationships

Transition changes things for partners too. Some relationships grow closer, some change shape, and some end, and each of those can be right. Honest conversation about what's changing in your body, your sex life and how you're seen as a couple helps. Couples counselling with someone experienced in gender diversity can help both of you, and partners can call QLife as well.

Work and study

The law is on your side

Federal law has protected people from discrimination because of gender identity since 2013, including at work and in education, and Victoria's Equal Opportunity Act protects gender identity too.

A transition plan

Many workplaces and universities will agree a plan with you: who is told and how, when your name changes on email and records, and how questions are handled. You decide how much to share.

If things go wrong

Keep a dated note of what happened and who was there. Talk to HR, your union or student support, and if that doesn't resolve it, you can complain to the Victorian or Australian human rights commission.

When people get it wrong

Being misgendered or called by your old name (deadnamed) hurts, and it can happen even with people who love you. It can help to decide ahead of time how you'll respond in different places: a quick correction ("It's Sam, actually"), letting it pass when you're not safe or not up to it, or a private word later. You get to choose, every time.

Your rights, in short

  • Discrimination because of gender identity is against federal law, and against Victorian law, in work, education, housing, goods and services.
  • In Victoria, practices that try to change or suppress your gender identity are against the law.
  • Updating your gender with Medicare doesn't change your entitlements.
  • Free legal help is available from community legal centres; Rainbow Door can point you to one.
